So here it is, cleaned up and expanded: a field guide to the machine economy as it stands in July 2026, written for anyone still catching up — and aren't we all.

I. The Magnificent Seven

Everyone in America can basically eat because of a handful of companies. We used to call them FANG, then MANGA; now the top of the market goes by the Magnificent Seven, and they practically hold up the S&P 500 with the majority of its worth. The newest permanent resident of that club — and the one this whole story orbits — is NVIDIA.

If you want to date the moment the broader public noticed, it's easy: ChatGPT's release, and the weeks after, when it reached 100 million users faster than any product in the history of the internet. That event repriced our economy, and NVIDIA has been trading places at the very top of the index ever since.

II. The graphics-card company

NVIDIA makes the graphics card sitting in my own computer. The academic story runs through people like Geoffrey Hinton and the early researchers who discovered that inexpensive consumer GPUs — the same silicon sold for video games — were dramatically better at the mathematics underneath machine learning than the CPUs everyone had been using. Graphics processors turned out to be ideal for the linear algebra behind transformer models: take folders and folders of information, compress it, "pre-train" on it, and out comes a large language model that can tell you what makes an olive an olive and not a piece of broccoli — and everything else besides.

The founding story is stranger than most people know. Jensen Huang got roughly $5 million from Sega on a trip to Japan to build gaming processors, and the company nearly failed anyway. They clawed their way out through arcade machines — the Mortal Kombat cabinets some of us were feeding quarters into — and then into home gaming. Today gaming is closer to 10% of the business.
